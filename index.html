The Truth About Childhood Immunisation in Australia

For years, Australia was one of the best-vaccinated countries in the world. Lately, the numbers have started to slip. This page follows the data to answer four questions: how far coverage has fallen, which vaccines, where, and how many children are missing out.

Chapter 1

Vaccines have already changed Australia's health story

Measles is one of the most contagious diseases, and it affected thousands of Australians in the early 1990s. As the vaccination program expanded, reported cases fell sharply.

Chapter 2

National coverage has fallen below the 95% target

The line chart shows national coverage from 2017 to 2024, and the ranking shows how Australia compares with other developed countries.

Chapter 3

The decline goes beyond one vaccine

The decline is not caused by just one vaccine. Coverage has fallen across several vaccines at the same time.

Chapter 4

Coverage varies across states, and within them

Immunisation coverage is not the same across Australia. The differences become clearer when comparing states, local areas, and children aged 1, 2 and 5 years.

Chapter 5

Behind the percentages are tens of thousands of children

A few percentage points sounds small. Here is what it means in children.

Chapter 6

Vaccine-preventable diseases still carry a heavy burden

Coverage numbers matter because of what they prevent. Here are the diseases behind them: how often they put children in hospital, how many cases there are, what they cost and what they can do.

Why the surge? It may be linked to falling immunisation coverage after COVID-19, reduced exposure during COVID-19 restrictions, and the natural outbreak cycle of whooping cough.

So, are we still protecting them?

Mostly, but less than before

Coverage has dropped below the 95% target and Australia has slipped down the world rankings.

Age 2 is the weak point

Every state fell furthest behind at age 2, and only 6 of 325 local areas reached the target.

Cities hold the most unprotected children

Western Sydney and Melbourne's west have the densest clusters, even where percentages look fine.

The diseases are still here

Whooping cough hit a record in 2024, and pneumococcal disease cost $68.2 million to treat in one year.
