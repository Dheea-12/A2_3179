Are We Still Protecting
Australia's Children?

For years, Australia was one of the best-vaccinated countries in the world. Lately, the numbers have started to slip. This page follows the data to answer four questions: how far coverage has fallen, which vaccines, where, and how many children are missing out.

By Dania' · Monash University · October 2026

92% of infants got all 3 DTP doses in 2025, down from 95% in 2017–2021
#24 Australia's world rank for DTP3 in 2025, after reaching #18 in 2021
28,477 two-year-olds were not fully immunised in 2016–17
95% the national target needed to stop diseases like measles from spreading

Two terms used on this page

Fully immunised: a child has had every vaccine due for their age on the National Immunisation Program schedule (checked at 1, 2 and 5 years old).

DTP3: the third dose of the diphtheria, tetanus and whooping cough (pertussis) vaccine, given at 6 months. Countries use it as a common yardstick to compare how well children are protected.

Is it every vaccine, or just a few?

A drop in one vaccine could be a supply problem. A drop across all of them points to something bigger, like families missing appointments altogether.

Chapter 3

Where are children missing out?

Australia is split into 31 Primary Health Networks (PHNs), the regions that organise local health services. Coverage is not the same in all of them.

A low percentage doesn't always mean a lot of children. A small region with poor coverage may have fewer unprotected children than a big city with good coverage.

What does the missing 7% look like?

Percentages can hide how many real children are involved. Here are the numbers behind them.

So, are we still protecting them?

  • Mostly, but less than before. Coverage has dropped below the 95% target and Australia has slipped down the world rankings.
  • It's broad, not one vaccine. Several vaccines show the same decline.
  • The 2-year check is the weak point. Every region fell at age 2.
  • Big cities hold the most unprotected children, even where percentages look fine.

If you're a parent, check your child's record on the Australian Immunisation Register.

Notes

  • The most recent regional (PHN and SA3) data in these tables is 2016–17. National and international figures go up to 2025.
  • WUENIC figures are reviewed estimates by WHO and UNICEF, based on Australia's official register data and rounded to whole numbers.
  • Maps use an equal-area projection so that regions are drawn at their true relative size.
